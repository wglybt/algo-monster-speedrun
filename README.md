## What AlgoMonster's Speedrun feature is

Straight from AlgoMonster's own description: Speedrun is **the third step of its 3-step system**. Instead of writing code for each problem, you're given a multiple-choice question about the technique or template that solves the problem in front of you. That's the whole mechanic.

The name is borrowed from gaming, where a speedrun means completing a game — or a section of one — as fast as possible. Same idea here: cover as many problems as you can in the time you have, by stripping out the slowest part.

What that means in practice:

- **No code editor.** You're not typing a solution. You're picking an answer.
- **No test cases, no submission, no runtime errors.** The thing being tested is your read on the problem, not your implementation.
- **Seconds per problem, not 20–40 minutes.** That's the point of the mode.

If you came looking for a timed competitive ladder, that's not it. Nothing about Speedrun is scored against other users.

## How a round actually goes

The flow is deliberately boring, which is why it's fast:

1. **Pick a section.** Speedrun is organized by pattern section — the Two Pointers Speedrun is one confirmed example, and there's a dedicated page for it.
2. **Read the problem statement.** Presented the same way as the problems in that section.
3. **Answer a multiple-choice question** tied to the techniques and templates that section introduced.
4. **Get immediate feedback** and move to the next problem.

That's it. The reason a session feels short is that step 3 replaces everything you'd normally do after step 2.

## Why the format exists (the official argument)

AlgoMonster makes four claims about why Speedrun is worth your time, and they're worth separating because they're not equally strong.

**The timing argument — this is the real one.** In a real interview, you'll spend at least half the time identifying the algorithm and explaining your approach before you write anything. Speedrun trains exactly that half. Most people preparing for interviews spend 95% of their practice time on the other half.

**Reinforcement.** Running a few more problems right after learning a pattern cements it. Seeing twenty variants of "sorted array, find a pair" builds a recognition reflex that five variants won't.

**Volume and luck.** There are only so many interview problems in circulation. The more you've seen, the higher the odds you recognize the one you get. That's not a rigorous guarantee — it's just a numbers game, and the format lets you play more of it.

**Time.** The stated goal, and the honest one. You get through far more problems per hour.

> Worth being clear about: Speedrun trains recognition, not execution. If your actual bottleneck is writing bug-free code under a clock, no amount of multiple-choice will fix that. Pair it with real coding practice, don't substitute it.

## Where Speedrun fits in an actual prep schedule

The mistake is treating Speedrun as the main event. It's a review tool that only works if there's something to review. A four-week version that uses it properly:

**Weeks 1–2 — Learn the patterns.** Work through the sections properly: read the explanation, understand when the pattern applies, take the template, solve the problems with your hands on a keyboard.

**Week 3 — Start Speedrunning.** Once you've finished a major section, run a Speedrun pass over it the same week. Twenty to thirty minutes per session. The point is retention: you just learned it, so you should be recognizing it instantly. If you're not, the pattern didn't land and you need to go back rather than build on a shaky base.

**Week 4 — Real problems, with Speedrun as a warm-up.** Timed coding practice on a whiteboard or in a plain editor is the main work here. Ten minutes of Speedrun before a mock interview is a warm-up for pattern recognition, the same way you'd warm up before anything else.

That's the 3-step system's logic, incidentally: learn the pattern, solve it in code, then hit Speedrun to lock in the recognition. Skipping step two and going straight to multiple choice gives you a skill you can't use.

## Honest limits

Things the format doesn't do, based on the official feature description and what independent reviews keep flagging:

- **It doesn't test your code.** No editor, no test cases, no runtime. Implementation is on you, elsewhere.
- **It tests recognition, not explanation.** You'll still need to practice saying your approach out loud, since that's the other half of the interview. Multiple choice doesn't rehearse that.
- **The problem pool is smaller than a full problem bank.** Reviews comparing it to platforms built around timed contests note the breadth difference. You're trading volume for focus.
- **No system design.** Every comparison write-up lands on the same gap — AlgoMonster is DSA-focused. Senior candidates need a second resource.
- **It assumes you've done the section.** The multiple-choice options reference the techniques and templates from that specific section. Going in cold is mostly a way to feel bad about yourself.

That last point is the one people get wrong most often, and it's the difference between Speedrun being a genuinely fast way to consolidate what you know and being a random quiz you keep failing.
